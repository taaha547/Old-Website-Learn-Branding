Introduction

It's July 7th, 2026. Portugal has just lost to Spain, 1-0. Within minutes, social media splits into two camps, each certain their man is the greatest footballer alive. The Messi-Ronaldo debate has run for over a decade without a single statistic settling it. Why? Because statistics were never really the point. Fans don't defend numbers but rather they defend identity. This exact contradiction is why we're willing to go to war over a phone brand such as Apple, but shrug at a bottle of dish soap.

Why do some brands become identities while others stay mere products? The answer has more to do with psychology than it does with marketing budgets. It is actually with how much a product can fuse itself with who we are.

Consumer researcher Russell Belk opened this line of thinking by arguing that our possessions blur into our sense of self. As he put it from his sources, "between what a man calls 'me' and what he simply calls 'mine,' the line is difficult to draw." Sirgy's self-congruity theory builds on this: we're drawn to products that match our actual self-image, or the image we're reaching for. (Actual, Ideal, Social, Ideal Social Self)

Erving Goffman explains why this matters so much in social situations or publicly. In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, he describes social life as a kind of theatre, where everyone is quietly aiming to control the impression they make on an audience, often to their favor. Pierre Bourdieu's Distinction closes the loop, showing how taste in things such as dress, speech, education, and even the brands we use are functioning as Cultural Capital, sorting people into in-groups of belonging and out-groups of exclusion.

Line those four ideas up and you get a rough sequence. What we own becomes part of who we are. So we reach for things that fit the person we are, or want to be. Then we show that choice off, so people read us the way we'd like to be read. And that showing-off is exactly what splits a crowd into people who get it and people who don't. A brand that fits this whole sequence stops being a product and it becomes a badge of honour or entry.

MacBook vs. Linux

Both exist to do the same basic job of running your computer, yet each sells a completely different self-image. A MacBook signals the polished professional: someone who wants a seamless, integrated experience and is willing to pay for it. Linux sells the opposite fantasy of having freedom, dominion, self-sufficiency. Inside the community, the running joke "I use Arch, btw" exists purely to signal expertise, since Arch is notoriously unforgiving to run. Neither camp is really arguing about processing speed. They're arguing about who they are and, much like Ronaldo's and Messi's fans, neither side is about to switch loyalties based on the facts since it shows the nuance that neither may be the best for everyone.

Knives vs. Dish Soap

Try this: list five brands of knives, then five of dish soap. One list probably comes faster, but neither should take you too long. Yet, you would have never seen a 150-message forum thread arguing over the best dish soap. That's because no identity rides on it. Nobody feels proud to say they use Vim Dishwash. But plenty of people feel a flicker of pride owning the knife set their favourite chef endorses. Both are just kitchen tools doing an unglamorous job. Only one of them lets you say something about yourself. One says I'm serious, the other says basically nothing.

A feature can persuade a customer for a day. An identity can recruit one for a decade.

For advertisers, this reframes their entire perspective. A brand, in Belk's terms, becomes a possession people will defend as fiercely as they'd defend themselves. The brands that understand this stop advertising specifications and start advertising the identities they want their product to be associated with — instead of "here is what our product does," they say "here is who you become by choosing it."

Conclusion

Which brings us back to Ronaldo and Messi, or rather, to the jerseys, boots, and energy drinks sold under their names. Nobody starts a flame war over dish soap, because dish soap asks nothing of your identity. A football shirt, a MacBook, a Linux distro; all of these ask you to pick a side. And once you do, defending that side becomes a way of defending yourself.

That's the real line between a product and a brand. A product cleans your dishes. A brand tells a story about who you are, and hands you people to tell it to. One stays an object; the other becomes a noun. The only real question left is: which one are you?
