For decades, brand-loyalty programs, "find your niche" advice, and stories about beloved brands have shaped how most people think branding works. Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) tested these assumptions against decades of real purchase data — and several of them don't hold up.

{% include components/quote-callout.html eyebrow="Core Finding" quote="Brands mostly compete for the same light, infrequent buyers — not for a devoted inner circle." source="Byron Sharp" citation="How Brands Grow, 2010" %}

## Mental and physical availability

Two things predict whether someone buys a brand: whether it comes to mind in a buying situation (**mental availability**), and whether it's easy to actually find and purchase (**physical availability**). Distinctive, consistent assets — colors, logos, sounds, taglines — are what make a brand easy to notice and recall, which is why they matter more to growth than most marketers assume.

{% include components/key-takeaway.html title="Distinctive, not just different" text="A brand asset doesn't need to be original to work — it needs to be so consistently used that people instantly attach it to your brand alone." %}

## The Double Jeopardy Law

One of the most consistently replicated findings in marketing science: smaller brands have both fewer buyers **and** slightly less loyal ones. This isn't a story about customer devotion — it's a mathematical regularity that shows up almost everywhere market-share data has been studied.

{% include components/stat-card.html label="Pattern" value="Near-universal" caption="Observed across categories and countries, not a one-off finding" color="primary" %}

## What this means in practice

- Growth comes mainly from acquiring new, often light buyers — not from squeezing more loyalty out of existing ones.
- Protecting and repeating your distinctive assets is not decoration — it's how mental availability is built.
- Being "different" matters less than being remembered and easy to buy.
